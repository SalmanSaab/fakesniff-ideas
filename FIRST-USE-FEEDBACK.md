# First-use session — Emiel and Marco

You get one shot at a naive first impression. Once they know how it works, that reaction is gone
forever. So run this before explaining anything.

Takes about 15 minutes each. **Do them one at a time**, not together — the second person copies
the first, and you lose an independent read.

---

## The one rule

**Do not help them. Do not explain. Do not defend it.**

When someone gets stuck, every instinct says jump in. Don't. The stuck moment *is* the finding.
Count to ten in your head. If they ask "what do I click?", answer "what would you try?" and stay
quiet.

If you explain the hub first, you have not tested the hub. You have tested your explanation.

---

## Before they touch it

Ask, before they see the screen:

1. **"What do you think this is for?"** (they only know it is a company hub)
2. **"What would you expect to be able to do in it?"**

Write the answers down. If what they expect and what it does are different things, that gap is
the most valuable thing you will learn today.

---

## Hand them the phone. Say only this:

> "This is our new company hub. Have a look. Say out loud whatever you're thinking, even if it
> sounds stupid."

Then say nothing for two full minutes. Watch:

- Where do their eyes go first?
- What do they tap first?
- Do they scroll, or wait to be told what to do?
- The first moment their face changes — confusion, or interest

Write down **the first thing they tap**. That tells you what reads as most important, which is
often not what we designed to be most important.

---

## Then give them jobs, one at a time

Do not say where anything is. Say the goal only.

1. **"Find something we could put on a t-shirt."**
2. **"Mark one of those ideas as interesting."**
3. **"Find out what the rest of us are working on."**
4. **"Add a new idea of your own."**

For each one write down:
- did they finish it — **yes / no / gave up**
- how long, roughly
- where exactly they went wrong
- what they said out loud while trying

**A failed task is a better result than a passed one.** It is a bug in the design that you can
now fix cheaply.

---

## Afterwards, ask these (in this order)

1. **"What did you think this was going to do, before you opened it?"**
2. **"What was the most annoying part?"** — ask it like this. "Any feedback?" gets you "it's nice".
   Naming annoyance gives people permission to be honest.
3. **"When would you open this? Give me a real moment in your week."**
   ⚠️ This is the important one. If they cannot name a specific moment — Monday morning, before a
   supplier call, when a design lands — then the hub does not have a job yet, and no amount of
   design fixes that. Better to find out now.
4. **"What would make you not use it?"**
5. **"If you could delete one thing on here, what?"**

Do **not** ask "do you like it". Nobody says no to your face.

---

## What to write down for me

Per person, just this:

```
Name:
Before: what they expected it was for
First tap:
Task 1 find an idea:        done / stuck / gave up  — what happened
Task 2 mark interesting:    done / stuck / gave up  — what happened
Task 3 see others' work:    done / stuck / gave up  — what happened
Task 4 add an idea:         done / stuck / gave up  — what happened
Most annoying thing:
When would you open it:
Would delete:
Exact quotes worth keeping:
```

Quotes matter more than summaries. "I don't know what I'm supposed to do here" is worth more than
"navigation was unclear", because it tells you how they actually think about it.

---

## Read the results honestly

- **Both fail the same task** → the design is wrong. Fix it. That is not their fault.
- **Neither can name when they would open it** → the biggest finding of the day. Purpose problem,
  not polish problem.
- **They ask for a feature we already built** → it exists but is invisible. A findability problem,
  which is usually much cheaper to fix than building anything.
- **They only comment on how it looks** → they did not engage with what it does. Push harder on
  the task questions.

## Fair warning about staging

The staging database is nearly empty: a handful of test ideas, no real work items. Home is a
dashboard of counts, so it will look dead. Tell them that up front, otherwise "there's nothing
here" will swamp every other bit of feedback and you will learn nothing else.
